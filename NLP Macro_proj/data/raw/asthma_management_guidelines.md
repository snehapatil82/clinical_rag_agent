# Global Strategy for Asthma Diagnosis, Management, and Prevention

**Source:** Global Respiratory Clinical Initiative Guidelines  
**Document Code:** GINA-RESP-2024  
**Scope:** Adult and Adolescent Pulmonology Practice  

---

## 1. Definition and Pathophysiology

Asthma is a heterogeneous chronic inflammatory disease of the lower respiratory tract characterized by respiratory symptoms such as wheezing, shortness of breath (dyspnea), chest tightness, and cough that vary over time and in intensity, together with variable expiratory airflow limitation.

Key pathophysiological features include:
* Chronic airway inflammation mediated by eosinophils, mast cells, and T-helper 2 (Th2) lymphocytes or non-type-2 neutrophilic mechanisms.
* Airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) leading to bronchoconstriction in response to non-specific stimuli.
* Airway structural remodeling including subepithelial collagen deposition, goblet cell hyperplasia, and smooth muscle hypertrophy.

---

## 2. Cardinal Symptoms and Clinical Presentation

Classic clinical signs of asthma include:
1. **Recurrent Wheezing:** High-pitched musical whistling sound heard during expiration (and occasionally inspiration).
2. **Shortness of Breath (Dyspnea):** Episodic difficulty breathing, often worse during exertion or upon waking.
3. **Chest Tightness:** Sensation of constriction around the thoracic cage.
4. **Nocturnal or Early Morning Cough:** Dry or minimally productive cough, frequently disrupting sleep.

*Clinical Red Flag Pattern:* Symptoms that are episodic, worsen at night or in the early morning, and are triggered by exercise, cold air, allergens, or viral respiratory infections strongly indicate asthma.

---

## 3. Diagnostic Criteria and Objective Confirmation

A formal diagnosis of asthma requires two elements: (1) a history of variable respiratory symptoms, and (2) objective documentation of excessive variable expiratory airflow limitation.

* **Spirometry with Bronchodilator Reversibility (First-Line):**
  * Reduced ratio of forced expiratory volume in 1 second to forced vital capacity (**FEV1/FVC < 0.70–0.75** in adults).
  * Positive bronchodilator reversibility test: **Increase in FEV1 of > 12% AND > 200 mL** from baseline, 10–15 minutes after administering 200–400 mcg of inhaled albuterol/salbutamol.
* **Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF) Variability:**
  * Twice-daily PEF diurnal variability **> 10%** over 1–2 weeks in adults.
* **Bronchial Provocation (Challenge) Test:**
  * Methacholine or histamine challenge demonstrating a ≥ 20% fall in FEV1 (PC20 ≤ 8 mg/mL) in patients with normal baseline spirometry.

## 4. Common Environmental Triggers

Patient exposure to the following triggers can precipitate acute bronchoconstriction:
* **Inhalant Allergens:** House dust mites (Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus), animal dander (cats, dogs), fungal spores, and seasonal pollens.
* **Respiratory Infections:** Viral upper respiratory tract infections (especially Rhinovirus and Respiratory Syncytial Virus).
* **Physical & Environmental Factors:** Cold, dry air, vigorous aerobic exercise, tobacco smoke, air pollution (particulate matter PM2.5, ozone), and strong chemical fumes/perfumes.
* **Medications:** Non-selective beta-blockers (oral or ophthalmic) and Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs / Aspirin) in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD).

---

## 5. Stepwise Pharmacological Management

Asthma management is stratified across treatment steps based on symptom frequency and risk of exacerbations:

### 5.1 Controller Medications (Anti-inflammatory)
* **Inhaled Corticosteroids (ICS):** Cornerstone anti-inflammatory therapy (e.g., Budesonide 200–800 mcg/day, Fluticasone propionate 100–500 mcg/day). ICS suppresses mucosal inflammation, reduces exacerbations, and prevents remodeling.
* **Inhaled Corticosteroid + Long-Acting Beta-2 Agonist (ICS-LABA):** (e.g., Formoterol-Budesonide, Salmeterol-Fluticasone). Preferred Maintenance and Reliever Therapy (MART) using Formoterol-Budesonide across Steps 1 to 5.
* **Leukotriene Receptor Antagonists (LTRAs):** (e.g., Montelukast 10 mg daily). Oral add-on therapy, particularly beneficial in allergic rhinitis or exercise-induced bronchospasm.
* **Biologic Therapies (Step 5 Severe Asthma):** Anti-IgE (Omalizumab), Anti-IL-5/IL-5R (Mepolizumab, Benralizumab), or Anti-IL-4Rα (Dupilumab) for severe refractory type-2 asthma.

### 5.2 Reliever Medications (Rescue)
* **Low-Dose ICS-Formoterol:** Preferred reliever across all severity levels because it treats underlying inflammation while providing rapid bronchodilation.
* **Short-Acting Beta-2 Agonist (SABA):** (e.g., Albuterol / Salbutamol 100–200 mcg as needed). Alternative reliever; *monotherapy with SABA alone without ICS is no longer recommended due to increased risk of severe exacerbations and asthma-related mortality.*

---

## 6. Acute Exacerbations and Danger Signs

Severe exacerbation symptoms require immediate medical intervention:
* Inability to complete full sentences in one breath, tachypnea (> 25 breaths/min), tachycardia (> 110 bpm), accessory muscle use, peak flow < 50% predicted, or oxygen saturation (SpO2) < 92%.
